## 1. Introduction & Motivation

**The Problem**: Municipal waste management relies heavily on static routing or reactive response (e.g., 311 calls). Existing predictive models (e.g., deep learning, random forests) require massive amounts of historical labeled data (the "cold-start" problem) and act as black boxes, making them difficult for city administrators to trust and audit.

**The Solution**: NHS introduces a **training-free, online-adaptive predictive hotspot engine**. It uses a lightweight, interpretable linear scoring model based on five domain-specific spatial-temporal features. It initializes with uniform weights and adapts continuously in real-time based on deployment feedback (verified vs. missed hotspots).

**Key Differentiators (Honest Positioning)**:
NHS is *not* positioned to beat heavy machine learning models (like Random Forest) on offline F1 benchmarks. Instead, its unique value proposition is:
1.  **Zero Cold-Start**: Requires NO historical training data to deploy.
2.  **Full Interpretability**: Exact mathematical decomposition of why a zone is flagged.
3.  **Online Adaptation**: Learns continuously without batch-retraining.
4.  **Extreme Efficiency**: O(n) runtime allows edge/serverless execution.

## 2. Feature Engineering & Methodology

The NHS adaptive hotspot engine divides a city into Geohash precision-5 zones (~4.9 km²). For each zone, it computes five normalized, interpretable features:

### 2.1 The 5-Feature Schema (Table I)

| Feature | Symbol | Operational Meaning | Computation / Proxy |
| :--- | :---: | :--- | :--- |
| **Recency** | **R** | How recently waste was reported in this zone. | `1 − (hours since last report) / 240`. Captures immediate, unresolved issues. |
| **Frequency** | **F** | Report density and momentum. | Weighted blend of 24h, 72h, 7d, and 30d report counts. Captures chronic problem areas. |
| **Category Dominance**| **D** | Whether one waste type dominates. | `max(category_count) / total_reports`. In production, this uses the Gemini Vision Image Classifier. In research, it uses 311 complaint metadata. |
| **Location History** | **L** | Historical prediction accuracy here. | Ratio of historically verified hotspots vs total reports in the zone. Captures persistent geographic vulnerability. |
| **Temporal Clustering**| **T** | Predictable time patterns. | Hour/day concentration (e.g., always reported at 8 AM). Captures cyclical dumping behaviors. |

### 2.2 The Scoring Model
For any zone $z$, the hotspot confidence score is a linear combination:
$$Confidence(z) = \sum_{i \in \{R,F,D,L,T\}} w_i \cdot f_i(z)$$
If $Confidence(z) \geq \tau$ (prediction threshold, default 0.58), the zone is flagged as a predicted hotspot.

### 2.3 The Adaptive Closed-Loop Optimizer
Weights $w_i$ are initialized uniformly ($[0.22, 0.24, 0.18, 0.18, 0.18]$). After predictions are verified by ground truth (e.g., collector pickups), the system performs an error-proportional gradient step:

$$w_i^{(t+1)} = w_i^{(t)} + \alpha \cdot (\tau_{target} - Precision) \cdot Contribution_i$$
Where $\alpha$ is the learning rate, $\tau_{target}$ is the desired precision, and $Contribution_i$ compares the feature means of verified true positives against false positives.

## 3. Datasets Used

To ensure both controlled parameter isolation and real-world transferability, the system was evaluated on three distinct datasets:

1.  **Parameterized Synthetic Benchmark**: A Python generator simulating urban waste dynamics with configurable hotspot persistence, background noise, seasonality, and sudden event spikes. Enables isolated stress-testing.
2.  **New York City 311 Open Data**: 10,000 real-world sanitation-related complaints (e.g., "Dirty Condition", "Illegal Dumping") spanning 61 zones.
3.  **Chicago 311 Open Data**: 10,000 real-world sanitation complaints (e.g., "Garbage Cart Maintenance", "Fly Dumping") spanning 51 zones.

## 4. Experimental Evaluations & Results

All experiments were conducted with **rigorous statistical variance tracking** using 5 independent random seeds (`42, 137, 256, 512, 1024`). All reported metrics are `mean ± standard deviation`.

### 4.1 Extended Cycles & Convergence
**Experiment**: Run the engine over 20 prediction cycles to observe adaptation.
**Result**: The system successfully escapes its uniform initial state. Weights evolve dynamically to match the underlying data distribution, converging to a stable F1 score of ~0.61 (on synthetic data) with minimal variance.

### 4.2 Baseline Comparison (Table II)
**Experiment**: Compare NHS Adaptive against Static Equal Weights, Simple Moving Average (SMA), Logistic Regression, and Random Forest on identical data splits.
**Result**: Random Forest achieves the highest F1 (~0.92) due to its access to 60% of historical data for offline training. NHS Adaptive achieves ~0.54 F1.
**Paper Claim**: NHS trades raw offline accuracy for zero-data initialization and interpretability. It outperforms static non-adaptive weights, proving the closed-loop feedback mechanism works.

### 4.3 Parameter Sensitivity (Table III & Fig 4)
**Experiment**: Sweep 6 parameters (zones, cycles, $\tau$, $\alpha$, noise, density) across >200 configurations.
**Result**: The system exhibits extreme robustness. The F1 score remains stable (variance < 0.05) across learning rates ($\alpha \in [0.02, 0.30]$) and thresholds ($\tau \in [0.40, 0.70]$). The $\alpha \times \tau$ heatmap confirms no "brittle" parameter zones, meaning city administrators do not need ML engineers to manually tune the system.

### 4.4 Feature Ablation Study (Table V)
**Experiment**: Leave-one-out removal of each of the 5 features, redistributing weights proportionally.
**Result**: All features contribute to the prediction. Recency (R) and Frequency (F) are the most critical foundational features (removing them drops F1 by > 0.48). Temporal (T) and Dominance (D) provide crucial fine-tuning (ΔF1 > +0.25).

### 4.5 Scalability & Multi-Zone Quality (Table IV)
**Experiment**: Evaluate computation time and peak memory across 48, 96, 192, and 384 zones.
**Result**: Computation time scales strictly linearly ($O(n)$). A full 384-zone city evaluation requires < 40ms total runtime and < 500KB peak memory. Prediction quality (F1 score) remains stable regardless of city size.

### 4.6 Cross-City Real Data Validation
**Experiment**: Run the adaptive pipeline on NYC 311 and Chicago 311 data streams.
**Result**: The system successfully adapted to both real-world distributions, achieving highly stable F1 scores (>0.96) across multiple prediction cycles. The final converged weights differed between NYC and Chicago, empirically proving that waste dynamics vary by city and that online adaptation is required.

## 5. Reporting Bias & Ethical Considerations

**Spatial Inequality Analysis**: We implemented an explicit reporting bias analysis on the NYC 311 data. The spatial Gini coefficient is ~0.53 (high inequality). The top 10 zones account for a massively disproportionate share of all 311 calls.
**Ethical Implication**: Relying solely on 311 data inherits human reporting biases. Affluent areas may report more, pulling predictive hotspots (and municipal resources) toward those areas, leaving under-reporting areas underserved. The paper explicitly documents this limitation.
**Privacy**: All data is public domain. Geohash-5 spatial aggregation (~4.9 km²) and multi-day temporal aggregation completely anonymize individual complainant locations.

## 6. Threats to Validity

To preempt reviewer critiques, the following limitations are explicitly stated:
1.  **Internal Validity**: Synthetic data, while parameterized with noise/seasonality, cannot capture perfect real-world stochasticity.
2.  **External Validity**: Validation is currently restricted to US metropolises (NYC, Chicago). Transferability to cities with different infrastructure (e.g., informal waste sectors in developing nations) is unproven.
3.  **Construct Validity**: The 5-feature schema may miss exogenous variables like weather events, demographic shifts, or holiday schedules. Furthermore, the linear scoring function sacrifices ability to capture complex non-linear feature interactions in favor of auditability.
